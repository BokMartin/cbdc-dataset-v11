I. Progress of the Bank’s CBDC Research Project

1. International foundational principles and challenges for CBDC

In recent years, the issue of CBDC has attracted considerable attention, mainly because people see considerable room for imagining what CBDC could offer. However, in exploring this new field, many theoretical ideals and benefits will face technical and practical tests and challenges one by one.

To this end, the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and 7 major central banks of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Europe, Japan, Switzerland and Sweden jointly established 3 “CBDC foundational principles”1: “do no harm,” “coexistence,” and “innovation and efficiency,” as the basis for issuing CBDC2.

1
Under the “CBDC foundational principles,” 14 core features have also been established for the instrument, system and institutional aspects to facilitate the achievement of the foundational principles. Instrument: (1) convertibility, (2) convenience, (3) acceptance and availability, (4) low cost; system: (5) security, (6) instant settlement, (7) resilience, (8) availability, (9) performance, (10) scalability, (11) interoperability, (12) flexibility and adaptability; institutional: (13) a clear and robust legal framework, (14) regulatory standards. (1) Do no harm (do no harm)

The central bank’s foremost commitment is that the issuance of CBDC must ensure that it does “no harm” to the implementation of public policy objectives, and at least does not interfere with or impede the central bank’s duties in maintaining monetary stability and financial stability.

In particular, deposit money in the banking system is a source of credit creation, while CBDC3 could affect banks’ existing payment business and cause an outflow of bank deposits. Especially during a crisis, CBDC could have an even greater effect on the outflow of bank deposits, because CBDC “digital runs” (digital runs)4 are faster, exposing banks to more severe liquidity risks. Therefore, how to strike a balance between meeting public demand and maintaining financial intermediation, and avoid affecting financial stability, will be a challenge facing CBDC.

(2) Coexistence (coexistence)

The convenience of the payment system is built upon a diverse choice of payment instruments. The purpose of issuing CBDC is not to replace other forms of money or payment instruments, but to “coexist” with and complement existing cash, commercial bank money, credit cards, bank cards, electronic payments5 and other instruments,

At present, payments in major economies such as Europe, the United States and Japan, as well as Taiwan, have all developed in diverse forms. How the introduction of CBDC can coexist, interoperate and compete fairly with these payment instruments is also a challenge to be faced in the future6.

(3) Innovation and efficiency (innovation and efficiency)

Programmable money may be a focus of future innovation7. In addition, cross-border payments in the existing financial system involve intermediary correspondent banks, with relatively complex procedures and high costs. CBDC may be one solution for improvement.

Although innovation opens up opportunities to develop CBDC, if it brings risks to the financial and economic systems and damages public trust in central bank money, the potential benefits of CBDC cannot be realised. Thus, “innovation and efficiency” have substantive meaning only on the prerequisite of ensuring “do no harm” and “coexistence.”

3. Progress of the Bank’s CBDC research and experimentation

In June of last (2020) year, the Bank completed the 1st stage of research on technical feasibility, gaining an understanding of the limitations of applying distributed ledger technology (DLT) [分散式帳本技術], particularly that its operational performance cannot yet meet the demand for real-time, high-frequency, high-volume payment transactions. At present, the 2nd-stage “General-purpose CBDC Experimental Project” is underway and has entered the technical experimentation stage. By building a CBDC prototype platform, experiments simulate the application of CBDC in retail payment scenarios13, with completion expected in September of next (2022) year.

III. Conclusion

Objectively speaking, there is no first-mover advantage in CBDC technology. Countries’ development of CBDC is not a race; rather, it is about ensuring that the issuance of CBDC must, at a minimum, meet domestic needs. The few countries issuing it first, in order to address their particular domestic payment-environment problems24, can indeed experiment with CBDC technology first, but must also bear the potential risks of that technology. Other countries have a late-mover advantage, including learning from their development experience, further preventing drawbacks and promoting benefits, and building a more comprehensive CBDC ecosystem.

CBDC needs to offer greater advantages than existing payment instruments to be worth introducing. The Bank will continue to conduct research and experiments pragmatically, continue exploring the future prospects of CBDC,

24
Emerging countries such as the Bahamas and those of the Eastern Caribbean have insufficient financial infrastructure, excessively high cash issuance costs, and poor results from private-sector promotion of electronic payments. Switching instead to central bank issuance of CBDC is one feasible option for governments to achieve their policy objective of financial inclusion. China is facing a private-sector payments monopoly that continues to crowd out the use of cash. As cash usage gradually declines, if the central bank can introduce CBDC, it could be expected to fill the gap left by cash, enabling the public to continue obtaining the safest central bank money within the payment system.
