V. Latest International Developments in Central Bank Digital Currency and Progress in the Bank's Research Planning

Driven by the COVID-19 pandemic, the development of the digital economy has accelerated. At the same time, the pandemic has also changed the public's payment behavior, making people more willing and more accustomed than before to using digital payment methods1. Such changes are very likely to continue after the pandemic, driving human society further toward a more digital future. Central banks internationally have also followed this trend by exploring central bank digital currency (CBDC), and are continuing to improve the foundations for research and development, advancing research or preparing experimental projects step by step. This article compiles the latest international developments in CBDC, and presents the Bank's survey of domestic businesses' views on CBDC and the current state of the Bank's research planning, in order to keep abreast of domestic and international CBDC developments and ensure that related progress keeps pace with international developments.

(I) Central banks of major countries and international institutions are continuing to improve the foundations for CBDC research and development

1. Seven major central banks and the BIS establish foundational principles and core features of CBDC
    －As national circumstances differ, countries' considerations regarding CBDC will also differ. However, central banks' responsibility to maintain monetary and financial stability is common to all countries. Accordingly, the seven major central banks of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the euro area, Japan, Switzerland and Sweden, together with the BIS, jointly established “CBDC Foundational Principles and Core Features”2 in October of this (2020) year, committing that any issuance of CBDC must not impair central banks' functions in maintaining monetary and financial stability.

Table 1 CBDC Foundational Principles
Item | Explanation
Do no harm | Do not impair central banks' policy objectives of monetary and financial stability
Coexistence | Ensure coexistence with, and complementarity to, existing forms of money (such as cash and commercial bank money)
Innovation and efficiency | Public-private collaboration to jointly promote a payment environment of innovation and efficiency
Source: BOC, ECB, BOJ, Riksbank, SNB, BOE, Fed and BIS (2020)

    －The “CBDC Foundational Principles” include do no harm, coexistence, and innovation and efficiency (Table 1), and are the highest guiding principles that CBDC issuance should follow.

2. Japan, Switzerland and the IMF, among others, have already explored legal issues concerning CBDC

As required by the “CBDC Core Features” described above, the issuance of CBDC must have a “clear and sound legal framework.” To this end, internationally, the Bank of Japan, the Swiss Federal Council and the IMF, among others, have successively published reports3 exploring the legal issues that CBDC may involve, as follows:

    －There should be a basis for issuance: there should be a clear legal basis for issuing CBDC.
    －Explore legal tender status [法償效力]: it is necessary to explore whether the law grants CBDC legal tender status, so that, as with cash, no one may refuse to accept it.
    －Clarify legal attributes: the legal attributes of CBDC (for example, treating it like cash or electronic accounts [帳戶]) should be clarified. This will affect legal judgments and how related legal procedures are carried out, such as the transfer and seizure of CBDC.
    －Determine the roles of intermediaries [中介機構]: the provision of CBDC services will involve many intermediaries (including banks and non-bank institutions providing payment-related services, etc.). It is necessary to clearly define

3. The European Central Bank proposes possible scenarios and requirements for its future issuance of CBDC
    －The European Central Bank published a report on a digital euro5 in October of this year, envisioning 7 scenarios in which a digital euro might be issued in the future, as well as 14 requirements that should first be met before issuance (Figure 1), as a reference for future assessment. However, the Bank emphasized that even if the envisioned scenarios actually occur in the future and the related requirements can also be met, this still does not mean that a digital euro will necessarily be launched; it must still be assessed together with other possible options6.

2. Mainland China launches digital renminbi pilots
     －The People's Bank of China positions the digital renminbi as a substitute for cash16, adopting a two-tier model [雙層式架構] in its operation; research began in 2014, and intermediaries [中介機構]17 were brought into joint research and development at the end of 2017. To date, basic research and development work has been completed, and internal closed pilot tests are being conducted in Shenzhen, Suzhou, Xiong'an, Chengdu and future Winter Olympics settings.

     －In October this year, the Shenzhen municipal government cooperated with the People's Bank of China to conduct a digital renminbi pilot. Funded by Shenzhen, 50,000 digital renminbi “red packets” worth 200 yuan each were distributed to local residents through a lottery. Winners could spend them at more than 3,000 designated local merchants within the time limit18, completing payments by scanning barcodes or using NFC, methods commonly used in mobile payments at present (Figure 2); in December, Suzhou also conducted a pilot using the “red packet” model. In addition to expanding the scale of red packet distribution and the number of participating merchants, it further tested the offline payment [離線支付] function19.

     －The Bahamas has a population of only approximately 400,000, spread across as many as 700 islands. Owing to a lack of payment infrastructure on the outlying islands, most residents can only rely on cash transactions. However, cash handling costs are high, particularly because transport between the islands is difficult.

(III) The progress of the Bank's CBDC research planning is consistent with international trends

      The Bank is currently conducting the second-phase “General-Purpose CBDC Experimental Project,” with plans to adopt a two-tier model25, relying on intermediaries to provide users with CBDC wallet opening and payment-related services. Therefore, to facilitate the advancement of the experimental project, the Bank's CBDC working group held an online briefing on the CBDC research project in August this year, explaining the planning of the experimental project to major banks, non-bank payment institutions and related businesses26, while exchanging opinions and views and conducting a written survey27.

1. The survey shows that domestic businesses have expectations for the future development of CBDCs
(1) Expectations that a CBDC can improve current pain points in domestic electronic payments
     －The Bank's compilation of responses from participating institutions found that cash is functioning well domestically, but physical cash cannot be used in a digital environment. Although domestic electronic payments are diverse and convenient to use, many pain points remain. For example: the domestic payment market is fragmented28, with poor interoperability affecting payment efficiency; there are barriers to use (for example, age restrictions on account opening; a bank account [帳戶], ATM/debit card or credit card must be linked), and merchants' considerations regarding fees and other costs (for example, merchants may be unwilling to adopt electronic payments because of fees or taxation issues, as well as considerations about the integration and costs of acquiring equipment) affect the promotion of electronic payments; in addition, they cannot be used offline like cash.

Preliminary planning mainly includes three types of pilot scenarios: (1) delivery versus payment (DvP) for large-value transactions30, (2) domestic consumer payments and transfers, and (3) small-value cross-border remittances31. These broadly cover the main applications of general-purpose CBDC. Subsequently, communication with relevant businesses regarding specific needs and detailed processes, among other things, will continue, in order to plan a design that can resolve current payment pain points and meet market needs.

     －In the future, the Bank will also seek opinions from relevant external institutions from time to time as required by operational planning, and adjust its planning at any time in line with actual future needs.

(IV) Conclusion

1. Major countries currently have no issuance plans, but have not overlooked the potential for future development of CBDC
     －With the trend toward the development of the digital economy, the digitalization of money is already underway. For example, the various online and mobile payments made by the public using commercial bank money and electronic money, and the various large-value payments made by banks using central bank money in the form of central bank reserves, have all been digitalized. However, another form of central bank money, “cash,” cannot be used in a digital environment, and its role as a payment instrument may gradually weaken in the future, creating a gap in the payments market.

     －Electronic payments in major countries are well developed and continue to improve. Therefore, none currently has plans to issue CBDC or has announced a timetable for its introduction. However, in view of CBDC's potential for future development as a digital payment instrument used in the digital economy, they have also successively begun research and pilots recently, to round out the possible options for future payment development. As for emerging countries lacking electronic payment infrastructure, the benefits of CBDC are clearer, and it can serve as an option for governments to resolve current payment pain points

2. The Bank's CBDC research planning is in line with international trends and domestic needs, and the general-purpose CBDC pilot project is continuing to advance
     －In addition to advancing their respective CBDC research and pilots step by step, central banks internationally have also worked together to formulate “CBDC Foundational Principles and Core Features” as the highest guiding principles for each country's CBDC, ensuring that CBDC issuance must “do no harm” to monetary and financial stability, “coexist” with other forms of money, and achieve optimal “innovation and efficiency” through a two-tier model involving public-private cooperation.

     －The Bank is currently conducting its second-stage “General-Purpose CBDC Pilot Project,” incorporating the internationally formulated “CBDC Foundational Principles and Core Features” mentioned above into its planning. At the same time, it will also test CBDC application scenarios targeting domestic payment pain points, in order to meet domestic expectations for CBDC. Current preliminary planning includes three possible pilot scenarios—DvP, domestic consumer payments and transfers, and small-value cross-border remittances—which broadly cover the main applications of general-purpose CBDC.

3. The Bank will continue to monitor domestic and international CBDC developments to refine its CBDC research and pilots
     －As a new form of central bank money, CBDC involves a very wide range of aspects. It may not only affect the central bank's responsibilities for maintaining monetary and financial stability, but may also have far-reaching effects on the economy, society, and other areas. Before deciding to introduce CBDC, all aspects must undergo in-depth and prudent examination, with an assessment of the associated benefits and risks and possible response measures, as well as broad discussion among the public, to ensure that the eventual decision to issue CBDC has achieved social consensus and has a solid foundation in all aspects, so as to continue the good tradition of widespread trust in central bank money.

     －Internationally, in addition to continued research and pilots on the technical aspects of CBDC, the possible legal implications of CBDC are also being explored. The Bank will continue to monitor international CBDC developments in all aspects and raise legal issues at appropriate times32. On the other hand, it will also, as needed, explain the progress of its CBDC research and exchange views with relevant businesses through briefings or other means, in order to continuously refine the Bank's research and pilots.
